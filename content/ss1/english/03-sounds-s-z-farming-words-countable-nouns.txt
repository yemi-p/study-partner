Phonation
Study Content

The airstream from the lungs must pass through the glottis. The glottis is a triangular opening in the larynx.
It is a door between the throat and the windpipe, the tube that leads from the larynx to the lungs.

The glottis is a triangular opening, which acts as a door between the pharynx (throat) and the trachea
(windpipe). It can assume quite a number of shapes or states during the production of speech. For example,
it may be open (such as when we are breathing), or shut tightly (such as when we carry a heavy object), or in
a state of vibration in which it opens and closes at extremely fast speeds.

The state of the glottis at the time the airstream is passing through it is very important for the sounds we
form in the vocal tract above the larynx (Adam's apple). This is very easy to demonstrate.

Words Associated with Agriculture

Farming is a universal occupation. In Nigeria today, almost everyone is a farmer. If you plant vegetables
in your kitchen-garden for home use you are definitely a farmer!

The basic farming implements are the hoe and the cutlass. The cutlass is used to clear the farmland
and also to get rid of weeds after the crops have been planted. The hoe is used for weeding but more
importantly, it is used to make heaps for planting root-crops like yam, potato, cassava and cocoyam.

These are mostly planted in the forest zone of the south and the middle belt area of the country. The hoe
is also used to make ridges for crops like groundnuts, tomatoes, okro and vegetables.

In the grasslands of the middle belt and the north, mechanised farming is more commonly practised.
Instead of hoes and cutlasses, tractors are used to clear large expanse of land. Grains or cereals such as
rice, millet, sorghum, wheat and maize are commonly planted.

The farmer often applies manure (animal-dung, poultry droppings and vegetable cuttings). These
enrich the soil and so the farmer is assured of a good yield or a bumper harvest.

With irrigation it is now possible to have two planting seasons instead of planting only during the
rainy season. Irrigation has also turned the arid land of the far-north into suitable farmland.

Whatever a farmer and his family cannot immediately consume or sell off is preserved. Tubers are
often stored in airy barns which are placed above the ground. These tubers may also be dusted with
chemicals which are harmless to humans before putting them on flat tops off the ground.

The traditional method of preserving grains is to hang them up by the fireplace. They can also be kept
in wicker-baskets and woven-grass barns. Nowadays, pesticides are applied to these grains before
storage. They can be stored in sacks, or air-tight containers. In the case of very large yields, they are kept in
grain silos above or under the ground.

Compare a drink and a bottle with two glasses and many boys. A drink means one drink, and a
bottle, one bottle. So, the indefinite article refers to only one object, in contrast to two objects as in
two glasses, and more than two, as in many boys. We say that drink, bottle, boy and glass are
countable nouns.

Because we cannot count ‘juice’, we say some fruit juice. We therefore say that juice is a non-
countable noun. Other examples of non-countable nouns are water (some water), beer (some
beer), soup (some soup), rain (some rain), sunshine (some sunshine), breakfast (some breakfast),
dinner (some dinner).

Notice, however, that some can go with both countable and non-countable nouns. Thus we can also
have some boys, some girls, some knives, some pens, etc. where some means a non-definite
number, usually more than two.

Now look at the following:

(a) | There was some sand on the kitchen floor.
(b) | Wehad some rice for supper.

(c) Titi found some bread in the cupboard.

Note that sand is made up of grains which may be too numerous to count. So when we refer to a mass
of sand we regard it as uncountable. However, when we wish to refer to more than one mass, we can
then use the plural sands.

Because we also regard rice as a mass we say some rice. Unlike sand, rice has no plural form at all,
but of course we can say a few grains of rice.

When bread is served at table we regard it as a mass noun, as in (a) and (b) above. However, in order
to make it countable we use the noun loaf and say a loaf of bread, many loaves of bread.

Identifying the Sub-topic/Theme of a Passage

By now your teacher must have corrected your homework. Did you get the topic/theme of the passage? In
this unit we are going to identify the theme/topic of a passage as well as its sub-topic/theme.

Sometimes a passage discusses the major topic only, as in the passage on force. A longer passage may
go further to explain the topic by giving an example or examples as we shall see in the passage below:

Anything that can cause a body at rest with some mass to move over a distance is a force. We apply force
if we want to open a door, move a chair or push a car. Have you thought of why things we throw up always
fall down? They are pulled down by the force of the earth called the force of gravity. The force of gravity
acts on every object on the earth's surface and above the earth.

You already know that the topic, theme or title of this passage is force. This passage also gives one
example of force. Can you identify it? There is a sentence where you can find this. They are pulled down
by the force of gravity. So the sub-theme of this passage is force of gravity.

What else have you learnt about summary work from this passage? It is that the topic of a passage can
be more than one word.

This is a clause (a group of words that has a subject and a finite verb and makes complete sense ifit is a
main clause).

Clauses are of different kinds: adjectival or relative, adverbial and noun clauses. They perform
different functions in sentences.

The expression 'who alone knew how to cure lunatics' is a clause that qualifies the village occult healer as
the most capable and efficient native doctor who can cure lunatics in the village and its environs.

It is an adjectival or relative clause. Therefore, an adjectival clause does the work of an adjective and
always qualifies a noun or pronoun; gives further information about a noun or describes it.

'The lunatic has broken loose again!' ‘Again’ from this expression is a word that suggests frequency, a
recurrence of an event, incident or happening. A situation that repeats itself or is about to repeat itself, it
goes with surprise, shock, wonder or astonishment.

'Again' as used in the sentence: 'the lunatic has broken loose again!’ suggests that the villagers have
experienced a similar case recently.

The emphasized words above seem to contradict the division we have made between countable and
non-countable nouns. All the nouns work, land, water, people, beer, food are often used as non-
countable nouns. But as the sentences above show, the plural forms can also be used to convey
certain meanings. Let us take the nouns one by one:

Work/works: work is anything one engages oneself in. It may consist of different activities, reading,
writing, gardening, cleaning the house. All these activities taken together are referred to as work.
After engaging in all these activities, you go to bed after the day's work.

Works, on the other hand, is commonly used in either of two senses. In sentence a (i) it is used to refer
to all of an author's writings. Thus the complete works of Shakespeare refers strictly to what
Shakespeare wrote and not to any of his other activities. The second sense in which the plural form is
used is to refer to public constructions. Thus we talk of the public works department.

Land/ands: We refer to \and in talking about the area of the earth around us. When talking about
different types of the earth's surface, however, we use the plural form. Apart from this, lands is often
used to mean countries. Thus we talk of people from other lands meaning people from other parts
(countries) of the world.

The distinction between water and waters is one between a small and often stationary body of the
liquid (water) and a large and often moving body (waters).

People refers to a collection of individuals. Peoples, however, refers to many different such
collections. We can talk of the people of Onitsha, even though not everyone in Onitsha is of the Igbo
ethnic group. However, we talk of the peoples of Nigeria, since the country is a collection of many
different ethnic groups. Hence, also, the peoples of the world.

Even when a man has had several glasses of beer, we say he has had a lot of beer. However, if we
concentrate on the units of beer served in the glasses, we can say that he had several beers meaning,

Narrative Essay (II)
Linking Paragraphs in a Narrative

In narratives, adverbial paragraph linkers are minimally used. The narrator's order of statements is
assumed to indicate the order of events. Consider this example:

‘I half opened my eyes. They met a dozen anxious eyes peering back at them. Mother was the first to
leap up for joy. 'Bisi has come round,' she announced excitedly. She wanted to hug me, but was held back
by a tall, slim woman in immaculate white, wearing an instrument round her neck. The anxiety in the other
eyes also changed to delight, in line with mother's.

My eyes, fully agape, dashed from one to the other, trying to figure it all out. "Where am I? Who are
