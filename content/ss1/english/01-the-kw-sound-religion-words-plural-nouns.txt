Introducing Pronunciation
Study Content

For some years now, we have been learning to write or spell English words. Therefore we must already be
familiar with the problems of writing English. We know that the same sounds are written differently. For
example, the letters underlined in each row below are pronounced the same way.

fair fare

not cough

bed said lead (metal)

Job (name) robe hope go
hail hale break

off laugh paragraph

flat plait

We also know that different sounds have the same spelling. For example, the letters underlined in each row
in the next set of examples are the same but represent different sounds:

prayer prayer (one who prays)

cook cool

loose lose

said paid plait

One basic principle, which we must recall now, is one that we already learnt during our first week, if not our
first day, at school.
These sounds, which we produce for the ears, can be written for the eyes.

From the examples above, we see that the sound produced by making the lower lip touch the upper front
teeth is written, among other ways, as f, gh, ph. Now, in spite of these different spellings, we can easily
learn how this sound is produced:

Try saying along f fffff...(not eff but f as in fffffar)

Now, face your partners in the seat next to you and let them observe your lips. Let us also listen to the
hissing noise at the meeting point between the lower lip and the upper front teeth. If we are now asked to say
f, we know how it is produced. Similarly, if we hear f and we are told to write it, we know how to write it.

In order to write a language we must have an alphabet. An alphabet is a set of letters (each letter is a
symbol) used in writing a language. The English alphabet is well known to us by now:

ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ

We have just seen the English alphabet in upper case — which we probably know as capital (letters). We
write it most of the time in lower case — that is, what we know as small (letters):

abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz

Did you learn to sing the alphabet? You should try itnow:

a b c d, e f g,

h i j k, 1 m n Co) p!
1 m n Co) Pp q r Ss t,
u Vv w x y z!

How many other alphabets are you familiar with? If your language is written, it must have an alphabet, just
like Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, and English. Let us write down the alphabets of our individual languages.

Perhaps you have had a few problems writing your language? This is not unusual. You may be an
authority in speaking your language, but you are not an authority in its writing. Unless we learn to
pronounce it well now, we may become good writers but poor speakers of English. In order to speak
English well, we can either stay with native speakers and gradually learn from imitating them, or learn the
Phonetics of English. Phonetics is the science in which we study the sounds of human language.

Some alphabets are better than others. That usually depends on how well the sounds of the language have
been studied and made the basis of the alphabet. Ideally, each letter of the alphabet should represent a
significant/important sound in the language. In such cases, we say that the alphabet or writing system is
modern. The English alphabet is not tailor-made for the language; so it is not modern, and that is the reason for
the problems we identified above. By contrast, Yoruba, like most Nigerian languages, has a modern alphabet.

In learning the phonetics of the English language, we find a phonetic alphabet, which properly
represents the sounds of the language, based on the principle of one sound, one letter’. Thus the word sieve

would be /srv/, just using forward slashes to enclose phonetic letters, each of which represents an
important sound in the language. It will be our goal, as part of this course, to master this specialised
alphabet and associate each letter with a sound and a spelling or spellings. Paper III (Three) of the SSCE is
designed to test your knowledge of English phonetics and, therefore, of its pronunciation, as well as your
ability to relate its chaotic spelling to its pronunciation.

We hope you appreciate, therefore, the value of Oral (spoken) English as part of this course. You will
soon find, anyway, that Oral English is exciting and you will be proud of your spoken English, apart from
passing your English well at the SSCE!

Identifying the Sub-topic/Theme of a Passage

By now your teacher must have corrected your homework. Did you get the topic/theme of the passage? In
this unit we are going to identify the theme/topic of a passage as well as its sub-topic/theme.

Sometimes a passage discusses the major topic only, as in the passage on force. A longer passage may
go further to explain the topic by giving an example or examples as we shall see in the passage below:

Anything that can cause a body at rest with some mass to move over a distance is a force. We apply force
if we want to open a door, move a chair or push a car. Have you thought of why things we throw up always
fall down? They are pulled down by the force of the earth called the force of gravity. The force of gravity
acts on every object on the earth's surface and above the earth.

You already know that the topic, theme or title of this passage is force. This passage also gives one
example of force. Can you identify it? There is a sentence where you can find this. They are pulled down
by the force of gravity. So the sub-theme of this passage is force of gravity.

What else have you learnt about summary work from this passage? It is that the topic of a passage can
be more than one word.

The vocal tract is where speech is articulated — produced. It is therefore important for us to know the vocal
tract well. In Unit 2, we mentioned certain organs of the vocal tract without referring to the vocal tract. The

speech sounds) is an articulator.

Read the comprehension passage again and note these expressions:

'..who alone knew how to cure lunatics'
(last line)

This is a clause (a group of words that has a subject and a finite verb and makes complete sense ifit is a
main clause).

Clauses are of different kinds: adjectival or relative, adverbial and noun clauses. They perform
different functions in sentences.

The expression 'who alone knew how to cure lunatics' is a clause that qualifies the village occult healer as
the most capable and efficient native doctor who can cure lunatics in the village and its environs.

It is an adjectival or relative clause. Therefore, an adjectival clause does the work of an adjective and
always qualifies a noun or pronoun; gives further information about a noun or describes it.

'The lunatic has broken loose again!' ‘Again’ from this expression is a word that suggests frequency, a
recurrence of an event, incident or happening. A situation that repeats itself or is about to repeat itself, it
goes with surprise, shock, wonder or astonishment.

'Again' as used in the sentence: 'the lunatic has broken loose again!’ suggests that the villagers have
experienced a similar case recently.

The emphasized words above seem to contradict the division we have made between countable and
non-countable nouns. All the nouns work, land, water, people, beer, food are often used as non-
countable nouns. But as the sentences above show, the plural forms can also be used to convey
certain meanings. Let us take the nouns one by one:

Work/works: work is anything one engages oneself in. It may consist of different activities, reading,
writing, gardening, cleaning the house. All these activities taken together are referred to as work.
After engaging in all these activities, you go to bed after the day's work.

Works, on the other hand, is commonly used in either of two senses. In sentence a (i) it is used to refer
to all of an author's writings. Thus the complete works of Shakespeare refers strictly to what
Shakespeare wrote and not to any of his other activities. The second sense in which the plural form is
used is to refer to public constructions. Thus we talk of the public works department.

Land/ands: We refer to \and in talking about the area of the earth around us. When talking about
different types of the earth's surface, however, we use the plural form. Apart from this, lands is often
used to mean countries. Thus we talk of people from other lands meaning people from other parts
(countries) of the world.

The distinction between water and waters is one between a small and often stationary body of the
liquid (water) and a large and often moving body (waters).

People refers to a collection of individuals. Peoples, however, refers to many different such
collections. We can talk of the people of Onitsha, even though not everyone in Onitsha is of the Igbo
ethnic group. However, we talk of the peoples of Nigeria, since the country is a collection of many
different ethnic groups. Hence, also, the peoples of the world.
