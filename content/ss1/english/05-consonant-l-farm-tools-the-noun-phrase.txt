Words Associated with Agriculture

Farming is a universal occupation. In Nigeria today, almost everyone is a farmer. If you plant vegetables
in your kitchen-garden for home use you are definitely a farmer!

The basic farming implements are the hoe and the cutlass. The cutlass is used to clear the farmland
and also to get rid of weeds after the crops have been planted. The hoe is used for weeding but more
importantly, it is used to make heaps for planting root-crops like yam, potato, cassava and cocoyam.

These are mostly planted in the forest zone of the south and the middle belt area of the country. The hoe
is also used to make ridges for crops like groundnuts, tomatoes, okro and vegetables.

In the grasslands of the middle belt and the north, mechanised farming is more commonly practised.
Instead of hoes and cutlasses, tractors are used to clear large expanse of land. Grains or cereals such as
rice, millet, sorghum, wheat and maize are commonly planted.

The farmer often applies manure (animal-dung, poultry droppings and vegetable cuttings). These
enrich the soil and so the farmer is assured of a good yield or a bumper harvest.

With irrigation it is now possible to have two planting seasons instead of planting only during the
rainy season. Irrigation has also turned the arid land of the far-north into suitable farmland.

Whatever a farmer and his family cannot immediately consume or sell off is preserved. Tubers are
often stored in airy barns which are placed above the ground. These tubers may also be dusted with
chemicals which are harmless to humans before putting them on flat tops off the ground.

The traditional method of preserving grains is to hang them up by the fireplace. They can also be kept
in wicker-baskets and woven-grass barns. Nowadays, pesticides are applied to these grains before
storage. They can be stored in sacks, or air-tight containers. In the case of very large yields, they are kept in
grain silos above or under the ground.

Passive Articulators and Places of Articulation
Study Content

We have already said that active articulators are the lower lip and the tongue. By contrast, passive
articulators are the upper lip and the roof of the mouth.

These broad classifications do not help us to identify exactly the place of articulation of a given sound. So
we have to identify points which are touched or approached by active articulators when sounds are formed.

Let us look at this a little more. It would not help if we were to be told that a certain sound was formed by the
tongue and the roof of the mouth. Consider the first sounds of pool, tool, and cool, for example. While the p
of pool can be seen to have been formed by the two lips, the ¢ of tool, and the c of cool, would both be
described in exactly the same way.

The problem is removed when we identify more places of articulation at various points along the roof of
the mouth. Try and identify these points as you go down the list.

1. upper lip

2. upper front teeth

3. teeth ridge (behind the upper front teeth).

4. the dome (between the teeth ridge and the hard palate)
5. hard palate

6. soft palate

Below each passive articulator lies an active articulator. For example, the lower lip (active) lies below the
upper lip (passive). All we have to do now is to identify each passive articulator with a scientific label. Such
a label or term will automatically suggest what the active articulator is as follows:

- bilabial sounds: m, p, b

These are sounds formed between the lower lip and the upper lip. Here are some examples:

m occurs in man, moon, meat
p occurs in pan, pool, pen
b occurs in ban, boat, bean

-labiodental sounds: f, v
These are sounds formed between the lower lip and the upper front teeth.
f occurs in fan, fool, farm
v occurs in van, voice, vow
It should be noted that this is one case of articulation in which the active articulator is not directly under the
passive one. The lower lip curls backwards to articulate against the upper front teeth.

The diagram above shows that in a noun phrase, the elements or items which appear before the noun
must appear ina fixed order.

In (i) thereis no item before the noun, which is a proper noun.

In (ii) we have an indefinite article followed by an adjective.

In (iii) only the article (definite) comes before the noun.

In (iv) we have the article (non-definite) followed by two adjectives.

When a noun is preceded by two adjectives one of which describes the six and the other the colour,
the size adjective must come before the colour adjective, so we have other examples like the little
red book, the huge white building etc.

In (v) we have an item indicating proportion followed by the definite article which in turn is
followed by two adjectives.

These two adjectives are different from the ones we saw in (iv) above. Here, the first adjective
indicates our attitude to the noun (bicycles) and the second indicates the age (new) of the noun. The
attitude adjective always comes before the age adjective.

In (vi) we have in addition to the kinds of items present in (v), the numeral (six). Where there is a
numeral, it must come before any of the adjectives. The only item which may occur between the
determiner and the numeral is the ordinal (first, second, third...). We can in fact further expand (vi)
as follows:

(vii) All the first six beautiful iron gates.

It is even possible to further expand sentence (vi) to include all the four kinds of adjective shown in
the table above:
(viii) All the first six beautiful large yellow iron gates.

In this noun phrase, the adjectives are arranged as follows:

(a) the first adjective ((beautiful) expresses attitude;

(b) the second adjective (large) indicates size;

(c) the third adjective (yellow) indicates colour;

(d) the fourth adjective (iron) indicates the material of which the noun (gates) is
made.

We can generalize by saying that in arranging adjectives before a noun, we start from adjectives
which subjectively describe the noun to those which more objectively do so.

In the example above, (a) beautiful is subjective. Other people may not agree that the gates are
beautiful; (b) Jarge is however less subjective. People are more likely to agree that an object is Jarge
than that it is beautiful; (c) yellow is even more objective. There is likely to be even less argument
over whether an object is yellow than over whether it is large or not; (d) by the time we come to the
last adjective iron there can be no argument at all. If the gates are made of iron it is something that
can be scientifically verified in the event ofan argument.

Identifying the Key/Topic Sentence of a Passage

Read again the homework passage in the last unit. The passage tells us about house-wives. What precisely
is the writer saying about house-wives? He is praising them. Therefore your title can be 'House-wives
deserve praise’.

In the passage there is a sentence which tells you this. Can you find it? It is the first sentence of the
passage. 'Those we call “house-wives” deserve praise’. This first sentence is therefore the topic or key
sentence of the passage. The topic sentence as the word 'topic' suggests, tells us what the passage is about. It
is the most important sentence in the passage. All the other sentences explain this topic sentence.
