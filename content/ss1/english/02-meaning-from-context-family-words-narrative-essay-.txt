Introducing Pronunciation
Study Content

For some years now, we have been learning to write or spell English words. Therefore we must already be
familiar with the problems of writing English. We know that the same sounds are written differently. For
example, the letters underlined in each row below are pronounced the same way.

fair fare

not cough

bed said lead (metal)

Job (name) robe hope go
hail hale break

off laugh paragraph

flat plait

We also know that different sounds have the same spelling. For example, the letters underlined in each row
in the next set of examples are the same but represent different sounds:

prayer prayer (one who prays)

cook cool

loose lose

said paid plait

One basic principle, which we must recall now, is one that we already learnt during our first week, if not our
first day, at school.
These sounds, which we produce for the ears, can be written for the eyes.

From the examples above, we see that the sound produced by making the lower lip touch the upper front
teeth is written, among other ways, as f, gh, ph. Now, in spite of these different spellings, we can easily
learn how this sound is produced:

Try saying along f fffff...(not eff but f as in fffffar)

Now, face your partners in the seat next to you and let them observe your lips. Let us also listen to the
hissing noise at the meeting point between the lower lip and the upper front teeth. If we are now asked to say
f, we know how it is produced. Similarly, if we hear f and we are told to write it, we know how to write it.

In order to write a language we must have an alphabet. An alphabet is a set of letters (each letter is a
symbol) used in writing a language. The English alphabet is well known to us by now:

ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ

We have just seen the English alphabet in upper case — which we probably know as capital (letters). We
write it most of the time in lower case — that is, what we know as small (letters):

abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz

Did you learn to sing the alphabet? You should try itnow:

a b c d, e f g,

h i j k, 1 m n Co) p!
1 m n Co) Pp q r Ss t,
u Vv w x y z!

How many other alphabets are you familiar with? If your language is written, it must have an alphabet, just
like Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, and English. Let us write down the alphabets of our individual languages.

Perhaps you have had a few problems writing your language? This is not unusual. You may be an
authority in speaking your language, but you are not an authority in its writing. Unless we learn to
pronounce it well now, we may become good writers but poor speakers of English. In order to speak
English well, we can either stay with native speakers and gradually learn from imitating them, or learn the
Phonetics of English. Phonetics is the science in which we study the sounds of human language.

Some alphabets are better than others. That usually depends on how well the sounds of the language have
been studied and made the basis of the alphabet. Ideally, each letter of the alphabet should represent a
significant/important sound in the language. In such cases, we say that the alphabet or writing system is
modern. The English alphabet is not tailor-made for the language; so it is not modern, and that is the reason for
the problems we identified above. By contrast, Yoruba, like most Nigerian languages, has a modern alphabet.

In learning the phonetics of the English language, we find a phonetic alphabet, which properly
represents the sounds of the language, based on the principle of one sound, one letter’. Thus the word sieve

would be /srv/, just using forward slashes to enclose phonetic letters, each of which represents an
important sound in the language. It will be our goal, as part of this course, to master this specialised
alphabet and associate each letter with a sound and a spelling or spellings. Paper III (Three) of the SSCE is
designed to test your knowledge of English phonetics and, therefore, of its pronunciation, as well as your
ability to relate its chaotic spelling to its pronunciation.

We hope you appreciate, therefore, the value of Oral (spoken) English as part of this course. You will
soon find, anyway, that Oral English is exciting and you will be proud of your spoken English, apart from
passing your English well at the SSCE!

Notice that all the (a) words above are names of persons. The person in each case does

words do have something in common. In fact, you can change them round in sentences (i) and (ii)

We therefore say that all the words in (i) to (v) in section 1.0 above belong to the same part of speech
or form class because so far as the meaning will allow, one of them can be used to replace another in
asentence. They are all nouns.

Now, let us compare the (a) words with the (b) words. The (a) words stand alone Ade, Ngozi, Lawal,
I, Dapo. These are words which, with the exception of J, are the names of people. We shall say more

about J later. These names of people are proper nouns.

On the other hand, the (b) words are in two parts, as follows:

(i) the /teacher

(ii) the /visitor

(iti) & (iv) the /book

(v) the /examination

Notice that in each case although the word in the second part does refer to a person or thing the word
is not the name of a person. Because of this, the word has to be preceded by the definite article
'the'. These words are known as common nouns.

So, words which are names of persons are not preceded by the definite article, but words which
merely refer to persons, and those which refer to things, are usually preceded by the definite article.

Names of villages, towns and countries are also treated like the names of persons. So we have:
(i) Ibadan is bigger than Jos.

(ii) Nigeria is bigger than Ghana.
(ili) Ikeja is in Lagos State.
(iv) Africa is not too far from Europe.

This is generally the case, but there are a few exceptions such as The Ivory Coast in West Africa and
The Hague in Europe.

Introduction

A narrative essay is a writing in which a story is told. Everybody at one time or the other, tells a story. For
example, if you return from school and either of your parents asks you what you did at school that day, what
you tell him or her is a narrative.

‘Oh, Dad! It was not an easy day for me. Almost everything went wrong. You know I left home late, as I
had to wash Uncle Joe's car, as well as yours before I left.

T arrived at school at 8.05 a.m. and was caught for late-coming. I was made to pick litter round the
school during the first period. Unfortunately, my class had a Mathematics test then and you know that is my
best subject. I missed that test, which will definitely lower my Continuous Assessment Score!

If your narration went that way, you would have written a narrative essay!

The introductory paragraph \eads the writer's father into the type of day he had. The next three middle
paragraphs serving as the body of the narration, narrate the events of the day that support the claim in the
introductory paragraph, ofa bad day.

The concluding paragraph sums up the writer's general feelings about the day.

Note the following:

1. The events of the day narrated are past, so past tense of verbs is used. You should use the same tense,

when you write your own narration.

2. The events are narrated sequentially from the start of the school day to the close of it. Your own

narration must be sequential.

3. | The introductory paragraph arouses the writer's father's curiosity to know why and how the day was
bad. Your own introduction must be interesting enough to arouse the same feeling of curiosity in

your listeners/readers.

4. The Body - Events of the day were discussed, paragraph by paragraph. However, two or more events

that are brief, can be merged in a paragraph. Point out the events discussed in each paragraph. The

body of your own narration must follow a similar pattern.

5. The concluding paragraph is brief and gives the writer's final feelings about the day. Your own
conclusion must be brief and satisfying.

Content of a Narration

Your essay at this stage should be up to 400 words.

A narration can contain Factual or Fictitious (imagined) stories, or a combination of the two. The
narration you have just read about a child's day at school, is a typical example ofa factual narration.

The passage contains five sentences which are muddled up, owing to lack of full stops. Put full stops at
the correct places, without looking at the one done below. If you do it correctly, you will have this:

They left for the market that afternoon. As they were near the market, they heard a shout. It sounded
near. They looked back. To their delight, it had come from a friend they were going to see at the market.

You will notice that the full stop appeared where there are Jong pauses. They have separated one idea
from another. In other words, a full stop ends a sentence. Note too, that wherever a full stop appears, the
word following it begins with a capital letter.

Other Uses of Full Stops
1. It is used to indicate that a word has been shortened. Examine these:
P.O.Box standing for Post Office Box.
e.g. standing for example gratia (for example).
NB. standing for Nota Bene (note well)
Mr. standing for Mister.
e.t.c. standing for et cetera (and so on).

2. It is used after the last word of an address, to show that the address has come to an end. e.g.
Government College,
P.O. Box 18156,
Abuja.

3. Itis used at the end of the date when written with the address e.g. 11th April, 1986.

Points to Note
(a) Afullstop marks a long pause, end ofa sentence.
(b) The word following a full stop must begin with a capital letter.
